A satellite records thermal anomalies over a landscape. A fire prediction model receives raster observations and predicts a map for the next calendar day.

Past daily observationsForecast modelNext-day fire probability map

Start by naming the target. In this course it is a VIIRS active-fire proxy, not a complete fire perimeter.

Try it yourself

A pixel has no detected active fire. What can you infer?

Reason through the question and every option

How to reason: Separate a sensor observation from a statement about the landscape. This label records whether active fire was detected in one observation window; it does not record the entire history of that pixel.

  • The land has never burned — not the answer. A burned pixel can cool and stop producing an active-fire detection. A label from one window cannot establish that the land has never burned.
  • No active fire was detected in that label window — correct. This is the claim the label supports: no active fire was detected in that window. It describes the recorded observation rather than proving the physical absence of fire.
  • The satellite had perfect visibility — not the answer. A background label is not a visibility or quality flag. Without separate quality information, it cannot establish an unobstructed, complete observation.

Take away: When describing a mask, say “no detection in this window,” not “never burned.” Check separate quality evidence before making a stronger absence claim.

Done when: you can describe the prediction target in one sentence.

Step 3 · Trace information

A date is not the whole availability story.

Your task: Check whether each input could be available when the forecast is issued.

Ask two questions about every variable: when was it observed, and when could the forecaster obtain it? A retrospectively assembled dataset may not preserve both.

Try it yourself

Predicting Day 4: which input needs special scrutiny?

Reason through the question and every option

How to reason: Place the forecast issue time on the timeline, then ask whether each value could have been obtained by that time. An observation date and the time a processed product becomes available are different facts.

  • Terrain available before the forecast — not the answer. Terrain explicitly available before the forecast passes this timing check. Its spatial alignment still needs checking, but it does not introduce tomorrow’s observations.
  • Day 3 fire observations — not the answer. Day 3 fire observations precede the target day and are candidate inputs. They still require an availability check: a delayed product might not have reached the forecaster in time.
  • Weather measured during Day 4 — correct. Weather measured during the target day uses observations from the period being predicted. Feeding it to an earlier forecast can leak future information. A weather forecast issued beforehand is a different product.

Take away: Check both observation time and availability time. The retrospective package alone does not establish an operational forecast pipeline.

The available WSTS+ package does not fully establish observation/availability timing and quality lineage. Treat this as a retrospective benchmark with controlled missingness; do not claim operational forecasting from dates alone.

Done when: you distinguish an observation date from an availability time.

Step 4 · Compare labels

Active fire and burned area answer different questions.

Your task: Distinguish an active-fire label from a burned-area label.

Active-fire detection

A thermal anomaly detected during a sensor observation. A previously burned pixel may no longer be active.

Burned-area product

Area identified as burned over a specified interval. It has a different temporal meaning.

WSTS/WSTS+ and the progression task of TS-SatFire do not share the same target definition. Similar-looking maps do not make their scores directly comparable.

Step 5 · Submit

Write a five-line forecast contract.

Your task: Write a five-line input and target contract.

  1. What observations enter the model?
  2. What is the last input day?
  3. What day and label type are predicted?
  4. Which information must stay outside the prediction path?
  5. What can this dataset not establish?

Use fire_24935867 with input date 2021-02-16 and target date 2021-02-17 as your running example. The Cedar patch is an optional simplified exercise. You will use the same input/target distinction in every later lesson.

Try it yourself

Can tomorrow’s label be used during training?

Reason through the question and every option

How to reason: Trace two separate paths: legal past inputs produce a prediction; the target is then compared with that prediction to compute a training loss. The target may affect learned weights through training without being supplied as an input for the example being forecast.

  • Never — not the answer. Supervised learning needs target labels to calculate the loss. Banning future-day labels from supervision would remove the learning signal for this next-day task.
  • Yes, as supervision for a prediction made from legal inputs — correct. The training label supplies the answer after the prediction is formed from legal inputs. Held-out labels can later score a frozen prediction under the declared evaluation procedure.
  • Yes, as an extra input channel — not the answer. An extra target channel would hand the network the answer. That channel would not exist when forecasting an unknown tomorrow, so this would be target leakage.

Take away: A label’s role matters: supervision during training, scoring during evaluation, never an input containing the unknown answer. Held-out labels must not become training supervision.

Done when: your contract names both the target and the information boundary.
